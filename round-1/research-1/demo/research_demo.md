## Research Findings

The prediction of the boiling point of water at altitude typically relies on the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) model to estimate atmospheric pressure, followed by the use of the Clausius-Clapeyron or Antoine equation to relate pressure to the boiling temperature [1]. The ISA model assumes a standard lapse rate of 6.5 K/km [2, 3].

When observed boiling points deviate from these predictions, it is often due to differences between actual atmospheric conditions and the ISA standard. Specifically, the barometric formula, which calculates pressure at a given altitude, is highly sensitive to the lapse rate [2, 3]. If the actual local lapse rate differs from the standard 6.5 K/km (e.g., due to temperature inversions), the calculated barometric pressure—and consequently the predicted boiling point—will contain errors [2, 3].

Regarding aerosol loading, while aerosols significantly affect atmospheric chemistry and cloud formation through solute effects (Raoult's law) [4], they do not measurably alter the boiling point of a bulk sample of water under standard atmospheric conditions. The solute concentration required to significantly elevate the boiling point would far exceed typical ambient aerosol concentrations. Therefore, deviations in boiling point measurements at altitude are primarily attributable to meteorological variability (e.g., pressure and lapse rate deviations) rather than aerosol effects or alternative explanations like non-hydrostatic pressure [1, 2, 3].
